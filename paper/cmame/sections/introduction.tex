\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Neural surrogates of finite-element analyses are trained to return, in one network
evaluation, the field that an assembly and solve would return, so that design studies,
optimisation and uncertainty quantification can query many configurations cheaply
\cite{Pfaff2021,Lu2021DeepONet,Li2021FNO,He2024GeomDeepONet,Zeng2025PCNO,Liu2026GINOT}.
Most are trained by supervised regression on reference solutions. Each reference
solution is a full solve of the system that the surrogate is meant to replace, so the
training set costs, before the surrogate has made its first prediction, as many solves as
it has instances and load cases; every extension of scope, to a new geometry family, load
regime or mesh resolution, costs more.

For linear elastostatics there is a training signal that needs no solve. The
finite-element solution minimises the assembled discrete potential energy
$\Pi_h(u)=\tfrac12u^{\top}Ku-F^{\top}u$, whose stiffness matrix $K$ and load vector $F$
every finite-element code assembles before it solves; evaluating $\Pi_h$ at a prediction
$u$ costs one sparse matrix-vector product. The energy of a prediction exceeds that of
the solution $\Ustar$ by half the squared stiffness-norm error,
$\Pi_h(u)-\Pi_h(\Ustar)=\tfrac12\norm{u-\Ustar}_K^2$, a classical identity of
finite-element analysis \cite{Ciarlet1978}, and this half squared norm is the strain energy
of the error's stress field. Minimising the energy over a set of training instances is
therefore exactly supervised regression in the stiffness norm, with the same gradients at
every point: a surrogate trained on the energy learns from a weighted mean-square stress
error, and no training instance is ever solved. We call such training label-free. In the
terms of machine learning it is unsupervised, although, with the solution implied by $K$ and
$F$, it is exactly the supervised regression just described. It is
not self-supervised learning in the usual sense, which learns representations through
pretext tasks such as predicting masked parts of the input; the self-supervised terms that
\ref{app:prereg} and \ref{app:e1} examine belong to separate experiments.

Energy minimisation as a training principle is not new. The deep Ritz method
\cite{EYu2018} and the deep energy method \cite{Samaniego2020} minimise a continuous energy
over neural trial functions, and later work trains operators and mesh-based networks
without labels, with residual or energy losses
\cite{Xu2024VOL,Eshaghi2025VINO,Lee2025FEONet,Dalton2023,Wurth2024,Wang2026PretrainFEM}
(\cref{sec:related}). That the energy ranks candidate fields is the Ritz minimum principle
itself. This paper puts the one identity to three uses for neural surrogates, the three
roles of the energy. As training objective, the energy gives supervised stiffness-norm
regression without a solve. As evaluation metric, the stiffness-norm error is a
compliance-weighted mean-square stress error that bounds the error of the von Mises stress,
and for one load case differences of energy rank predictions by it exactly, without
reference solutions; its normalised form, the relative energy gap of our tables, needs the
solution's energy and is computed on validation instances only. As failure detector, the
sign of the energy shows whether a prediction is worse than the zero field, and a
closed-form rescaling ensures that it is not.

What label-free training buys is tested against supervision. Single runs of the same
network trained with and without reference solutions have given opposite outcomes
\cite{MonteiroFernandes2025,Sun2026FEVessel}; here the same network is trained on the same
instances with and without their solutions, over three seeds, under criteria fixed before
the runs, with the criteria that failed reported alongside those that were met. By the
identity above, the comparison is between two losses: the squared stiffness-norm error,
which the energy gives without a solve, and the usual relative displacement error, which
needs the solutions; the labels add no information that $K$ and $F$ do not already carry.
In two dimensions, a third network, supervised in the stiffness norm, separates the norm of
the loss from the labels. The claim of this paper is therefore not that labels are
uninformative, but that the norm in which one can train without labels is the one that
controls the stresses, and that supervised training in the usual displacement norm controls
them less well. The paper extends a companion note \cite{CaoSong2026}, which stated the
exactness identity, the conditioning bounds and their executable checks, and first reported
the main results of the July run (\ref{app:july}).

\Cref{sec:theory} derives the three roles from \cref{lem:exact}; \cref{sec:results3d}
examines them in turn on three-dimensional meshes, and \cref{sec:results2d} tests, in two
dimensions, the explanation that \cref{sec:theory} gives for what \cref{sec:results3d}
finds. The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Theory (\cref{sec:theory}). \Cref{lem:exact}, the identity of the squared stiffness
norm with the compliance-weighted stress norm and the Chebyshev bound of conjugate
gradients are classical \cite{Ciarlet1978,Saad2003}; we derive from them the following
statements for surrogates. The label-free objective is supervised stiffness-norm regression
(\cref{cor:training}), and for a homogeneous isotropic material the energy gap bounds the
error of the von Mises field (\cref{prop:stress}). The relative energy gap is the squared
relative displacement error times a factor that measures where the error lies in the
spectrum of $K$ (\cref{rem:factor}); displacement and stiffness-norm errors can rank two
predictions in opposite orders only when their displacement errors differ by less than the
square root of the condition number of $K$, and then they can (\cref{rem:rank}); and the
energy ranks predictions, and on average surrogates, by their stiffness-norm errors
without reference solutions (\cref{cor:rank}). The sign of $\Pi_h(u)$ tells, for each load
case, whether a prediction is worse than the zero field (\cref{cor:zero}), and the two terms
of $\Pi_h(u)$ give a rescaling after which it is not (\cref{prop:amp}). Under the Chebyshev
bound, a warm start lowers the iteration bound of conjugate gradients by a fraction that
depends only on the prediction's relative energy gap and the target tolerance
(\cref{cor:warm}).
\item Three dimensions (\cref{sec:results3d}). A transformer trained on the energy alone
was more accurate on all five metrics, in every seed, than the same transformer trained on
the solutions of the same 1,024 instances, and the pre-registered accuracy condition on the
label-free network was met with a wide margin. In the same run, a graph network trained on
those solutions had lower displacement errors than the label-free transformer on most
instances but higher von Mises errors on almost all of them, so that the two errors ranked
the networks in opposite orders; this is a reading of the run, not a pre-registered
criterion. Every supervised network returned some predictions worse than the zero field; the
label-free network returned none, averaged over each instance's load cases, and
\numThreeFreeLoadWorse{} in \numThreeFreeLoads{} load cases taken singly.
\item Two dimensions (\cref{sec:results2d}). The label-free transformer's displacement error
was \numCmFreeDispExcessAbs{} larger than the supervised transformer's, and it was more
accurate on every other metric. Trained on the stiffness-norm error instead of the
displacement error, with the same labels, the supervised transformer lowered its von Mises
error by \numHTwoBRel{} (a pre-registered hypothesis, supported), and its errors lay lower
in the spectrum of the stiffness matrix, as measured afterwards.
\item Negative results (\cref{sec:transfer,sec:cost}, \ref{app:e1}). No network transferred
to a three-dimensional mesh about twice as fine without retraining; a closed-form rescaling
applied after the fact removed about two-thirds of the label-free network's displacement
error there. In two dimensions, conjugate gradients started from a label-free prediction did
not need fewer iterations to a tight residual tolerance than from zero. A token-bottleneck
network that trained \numTimeBottleneckSpeedup{} times faster was less accurate and was
dropped.
\item Cost (\cref{sec:cost}). The label-free network was trained without a single solve;
in three dimensions the supervised networks' 1,024 labelled training instances took
\numThreeTrainSolves{} solves, and evaluating any method on the in-band and fine-mesh
validation sets took a further \numThreeEvalSolves{}.
\end{enumerate}

We do not claim that a surrogate is faster than solving a linear elastostatic problem; at
the sizes studied here, conjugate gradients reached the surrogate's accuracy within a few
times its inference time (\cref{sec:cost:inference}). Linear elastostatics
is the test bed on which the exactness of the objective can be proved and the comparison
with supervision kept narrow: the same network, the same instances and the same schedule,
with only the loss and its learning rate differing. The interest of label-free training lies
where solves are expensive and labels scarce, for example in nonlinear and time-dependent
problems whose implicit steps are again energy minimisations (\cref{sec:discussion}).
