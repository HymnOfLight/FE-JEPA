\section{Cost and warm starts}\label{sec:cost}

\subsection{Labels}\label{sec:cost:labels}

In two dimensions the comparison of \cref{sec:results2d} used \numCmSolves{} solves,
\numCmSolvesVal{} for the 256 validation instances and \numCmSolvesTrain{} for the 1,024
labelled training instances, four load cases each; the July run used the same labelled
instances and a further \numTwoSolvesRun{} solves for the coarsened validation sets of its
cross-resolution test (\ref{app:july}). In three dimensions the comparison consumed
\numThreeSolves{}: \numThreeTrainSolves{} for the 1,024 labelled training instances,
\numThreeFewSolves{} for the 64 fine-mesh instances reserved for few-shot training and
\numThreeEvalSolves{} for the in-band and fine-mesh validation sets. The validation solves
are needed to evaluate any method; the training solves are the price of supervision, and
the label-free transformer needed none of them.

\subsection{Training}\label{sec:cost:training}

Label-free training of the three-dimensional transformer took \numTimeThreeSeed{} h per
seed on one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090, for 204,800 steps; the supervised transformer costs
the same per step, plus its labels. Full self-attention over all nodes dominates this cost
and grows with the square of the number of nodes: one training step took
\numEtwoTransformerFineStep{} s on a fine-mesh instance of \numEtwoNodesFine{} nodes. In
two dimensions, with the later code, three label-free seeds trained in parallel on one GPU
of the same type took \numTimeTwoParallel{}. The token-bottleneck variant, which applies
attention to $M=512$ or $M=1{,}024$ pooled tokens, trained \numTimeBottleneckSpeedup{}
times faster per seed but was less accurate, and was dropped under its pre-registered
accuracy criterion (\ref{app:e1}).

\subsection{Warm starts}\label{sec:cost:warm}

\Cref{cor:warm} bounds what a warm start can save. For the prediction tested below, with an
initial relative energy gap $g_0=\numKfiveStartGap$, the fraction \eqref{eq:warm} is
\numWarmFractionSeven{} for $\bar g=10^{-7}$, \numWarmFractionTwelve{} for
$\bar g=10^{-12}$ and \numWarmFractionSeventeen{} for $\bar g=10^{-17}$. A residual test
$\norm{r_k}_2\le\tau\norm{F}_2$ with $\tau=10^{-6}$ corresponds to targets in this range:
it guarantees $g\le\kappa\tau^2$, which is up to about \numKappaTauTwo{} for the
two-dimensional condition numbers of \cref{sec:methods:data}, and an iterate exactly at the
threshold has $g\ge\tau^2/\kappa$, which is down to about \numTauSqKappaTwo{}.

The July run tested a warm start under criterion K5. On \numKfiveEval{} validation
instances and four load cases each, unpreconditioned conjugate gradients needed on average
\numKfiveZero{} iterations from the zero field to reach a relative residual of
\numKfiveTol{}, \numKfiveFree{} from the prediction of a label-free transformer of the
earlier code, trained for 100 epochs for this test, and \numKfiveNaive{} from the scaled
polynomial: savings of \numKfiveSaving{} and \numKfiveNaiveSaving{} against the 20\%
required, so that K5 was triggered and the claim withdrawn. The prediction had a relative
energy gap of \numKfiveStartGap{}, which five and twenty iterations from it lowered to
\numKfiveFiveGap{} and \numKfiveTwentyGap{}, but it did not shorten the run to the
tolerance. This is consistent with \cref{cor:warm,rem:residual}: at a tight tolerance a
warm start of this accuracy lowers the iteration bound by only a small fraction, and a
residual-based stop can see a prediction accurate in energy as further from convergence
than the zero field. Neither the initial residuals nor energy-based stopping were measured,
so the two effects cannot be separated here. The test used one network of the earlier code,
trained for half the usual number of epochs, and one solver without preconditioning.

\Cref{tab:cost} times the label-free transformer's inference against solves of the same
systems, on the same instances, with the sparse direct solver that produced the labels and
with conjugate gradients; the network ran on the GPU and the solvers on the CPU of the same
machine, and assembly is excluded from both. In two dimensions the direct solve took
\numCostTwoDirectRatio{} times as long as the surrogate's first call, as the median over the
instances of the ratio of the two times, and its solution is exact. In three dimensions this
median ratio was \numCostThreeValDirectRatio{} on the validation instances and
\numCostThreeFineDirectRatio{} on the fine-mesh instances, where the surrogate's median
relative energy gap was \numCostThreeFineGap{} against \numCostThreeValGap{} on the in-band
validation instances. Conjugate gradients from the zero field, stopped at the first iterate
as accurate as the prediction in the stiffness norm, took a median of
\numCostThreeValMatchRatio{} times the surrogate's first call on the three-dimensional
validation instances and \numCostThreeFineMatchRatio{} times on the fine-mesh ones, again
per instance; this stop reads the reference solution, so a practical run would take at
least as long. Started from the prediction instead of the zero field, conjugate gradients to
a relative residual of \numCostCgTol{} needed between \numCostWarmMore{} more and
\numCostWarmFewer{} fewer iterations (medians per set), consistent with
\cref{cor:warm,rem:residual}; as in \cref{sec:cost:warm}, the initial residuals were not
recorded. The solvers ran with \numCostThreads{} threads. On a second host, in three
dimensions, the direct solve took the same time with one thread, and conjugate gradients
\numThreadsCgGainMin{} to \numThreadsCgGainMax{} less, so the conjugate-gradient times here
may be overstated (\cref{tab:cost}). We do not read these times as a speed advantage of the
surrogate: the network ran on a GPU and the solvers on a CPU, neither the general sparse LU
factorisation of SuperLU nor unpreconditioned conjugate gradients is the solver of choice
for these symmetric positive definite systems, and the ratio of the matched solve to the
surrogate fell from \numCostThreeValMatchRatio{} to \numCostThreeFineMatchRatio{} on the
finer mesh (\cref{sec:intro}).

The labels themselves are cheap here. At the median times of \cref{tab:cost}, the
\numThreeTrainSolves{} solves that label the 1,024 training instances in three dimensions
take about \numCostLabelMin{} min of the direct solver, against \numTimeThreeSeed{} h of
training per seed, which the supervised transformer needs as well; for these problems,
label-free training saves those minutes, not the cost of training. Set against the direct
solve, the training of one seed is recovered after about \numCostBreakEvenDirect{}
predicted instances, and after about \numCostBreakEvenMatch{} against conjugate gradients
stopped at the surrogate's accuracy, an upper bound since that stop reads the reference
solution, with the hardware of \cref{tab:cost}. Label-free
training pays for itself where a solve is expensive enough (\cref{sec:discussion}).
